# Plain-English Evaluation & Recommendations

**Graduate Challenge — Options A through E**

> This document explains, in plain English, what each option means, what's good about it, what's bad about it, and why. It covers the thought process behind the evaluation, assessment, analysis, and final conclusions for every option on the table. No jargon, no spreadsheets — just clear reasoning.

---

## The Big Picture (Start Here)

Springbok Creek mine stops producing coal at the end of 2031. When it closes, the company faces a **$900 million bill** to shut everything down, clean up the site, and look after it for 20 years after closure. On top of that, the town of Dustyroo Flats — which depends almost entirely on the mine — would collapse, and 800+ people would lose their jobs.

The board has five options on the table. Each one is a different answer to the same question: **"What do we do after Springbok closes?"**

Here's the short version:

| Option | One-sentence summary | Verdict |
|--------|----------------------|---------|
| **A: Brave Blossom Full Go** | Build a new underground mine next door that runs for 20 more years | ✅ Recommended — but staged, not all-at-once |
| **B: Do Nothing** | Close everything, pay the $900M, walk away | ❌ Rejected — financially destructive |
| **C: Phased Go** | Same as Option A, but with checkpoints where you can bail out | ✅ Best version of A — lowest risk |
| **D: Buy Bronco's Lease** | Buy the neighbouring mine's land to get more coal | ⚠️ Keep as a backup — they won't sell |
| **E: Go Exploring** | Spend a little looking for new coal deposits | ⚠️ Worth doing alongside other options, not alone |

Let's go through each one properly.

## Option A: Brave Blossom — Full Go

### What it is

Brave Blossom is a new underground mine that would sit right next to the existing Springbok operation. It targets the same coal seam system (the Blackwater Group) and would produce about 6 million tonnes per year of hard coking coal — the kind used for steelmaking, not power generation. It would run for roughly 20 years (2033 to 2052).

It costs about **$389 million** to build (or ~$674 million if you add a 30% safety buffer for cost overruns). The longwall — the giant machine that actually cuts the coal — alone costs $190 million and is the single biggest expense.

### What's good about it (the case FOR)

1. **It makes serious money.** The project has an estimated Net Present Value (NPV) of **$997 million** on its own, or **$1,277 million** when combined with Springbok's remaining years. That's a strong return on a $389 million investment. The estimated Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is about 52% — well above what most mining projects achieve.

2. **It pushes the $900 million closure bill 20 years into the future.** This is the single biggest financial lever. Instead of paying $900M between 2031 and 2050 (with no income), you push that bill to after 2052 — after 20 years of revenue. Because of the time value of money, deferring that bill is worth about **$508 million in today's dollars**. In fact, for every $1 you spend building Brave Blossom, you defer $2.31 of closure liability. Even if the mine produced zero coal, you'd still come out ahead — about $185 million net positive — just from pushing the closure bill out.

3. **It saves 800+ jobs.** The Springbok workforce has the right skills for Brave Blossom — same geology, same coal, same equipment type. Rather than firing everyone and recruiting from scratch in a few years, the workforce transitions across.

4. **It keeps Dustyroo Flats alive.** The town depends on the mine. Without Brave Blossom, it's an economic collapse. With it, the town has 20 more years of income, housing, and community.

5. **75% of the mine plan already has approval.** Three-quarters of the area sits within the existing Mining Lease — state approval is already in place. Only 25% sits on a different type of lease (a Mineral Development Licence) that needs federal sign-off.

6. **The customers are already there.** The Japanese joint venture partners are steelmakers who need hard coking coal. They've been briefed on the slightly lower coal quality and are expected to keep buying.

7. **The coal is the right type.** This is hard coking coal (used for steel), not thermal coal (used for power). Coking coal demand is driven by global steel production, not the energy transition. The price benchmark used ($240/t USD) is already conservative — below the historical range of $295–360/t.

### What's bad about it (the case AGAINST)

1. **25% of the mine plan needs federal approval — and that's uncertain.** The federal government could refuse or delay approval for the Mineral Development Licence portion. If they say no, the mine shrinks from a 20-year life to about 15 years. Still viable, but significantly less valuable.

2. **The coal quality is worse than Springbok's.** Brave Blossom's coal carries a **12% price discount** — it sells for $211.20/t instead of the $240/t benchmark. The coal seam thins as you move east, which means more waste rock mixed in and lower quality. If the actual quality is worse than expected, that discount could widen to 15–18%, which would cut the NPV by roughly $80 million per 5% of additional discount. The project is still positive at 18% (~$859M NPV), but it's a real erosion of value.

3. **The timeline is brutal.** Work must start in 2027. Springbok closes at the end of 2031. Brave Blossom's first coal is around 2033. That's a 2-year gap with no revenue while you're spending $221.5 million in a single year (2032) on the longwall and equipment. If anything slips — approvals, drilling, equipment delivery — the gap widens and costs pile up.

4. **$389 million is a concept estimate, not a firm price.** Concept-stage estimates typically grow by 20–30% as you learn more. At +30%, the capital jumps to ~$506 million, and that's before counting working capital, sustaining capital over 20 years, or owner's costs. The true all-in number could be $500M+.

5. **The rail and port capacity might not be there.** The current contract gives them 4 million tonnes per year of rail/port capacity through 2028. Brave Blossom needs 6 million tonnes — a 50% increase. Two new competing mines are opening on the same rail line from 2028. If they can't secure the extra capacity, the project is throttled.

6. **The CHPP (coal processing plant) reaches end of life in 2032.** The plant that processes the coal is aging. It needs $24 million in life-extension work right when peak capital is being spent. If the plant can't be extended, they'd need a new one — far more expensive.

7. **Old workings are sealed and their condition is unknown.** The previous operator sealed parts of the mine in the early 2000s after persistent roof falls. Water ingress, roof stability, and gas levels in those sealed areas are all unknown. Re-entering old workings is inherently risky.

8. **Carbon costs are real and rising.** The model accounts for about $200M+ in carbon compliance costs over the mine life using an "Accelerated Transition" price curve ($45/t rising to $200/t). But decommissioned mine emissions (4.68 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent over 20 years post-closure) may not be fully captured in the headline NPV. At a mid-range carbon price of $150/t, that's a potential ~$700M additional liability.

9. **Private landholders could block the last 4 panels.** The final panels of the mine plan run under private land. Subsidence from underground mining could trigger disputes, legal action, or political pressure.

### Why the good outweighs the bad

The strongest argument isn't the $997M NPV — it's the **$1-to-$2.31 ratio**. Even if every single risk materialises — approval refused, quality worse than expected, costs blow out, carbon prices spike — the project still defers the $900M closure bill. The deferral alone creates value. And the stage-gated approach (see Option C) means the board never has to bet the full $389M before knowing whether the risks are real.

### Final assessment: ✅ RECOMMENDED (via staged approach — see Option C)

## Option B: Do Nothing (Closure Only)

### What it is

Close Springbok at the end of 2031. Pay the full $900 million closure cost. Demolish everything. Sell off or demolish 505 company houses. Walk away from Dustyroo Flats. Generate zero revenue from 2031 onwards.

### What's good about it (the case FOR)

1. **Zero new capital.** No $389M to find. No longwall to order. No mine to build. You just close.

2. **Simplest execution.** No federal approvals to chase. No rail capacity to negotiate. No equipment lead times. No workforce to transition. Closure is a well-understood process.

3. **No project execution risk.** You're not betting on coal prices, approval timelines, geotechnical conditions, or equipment delivery. You're just shutting down.

4. **Clear, fixed timeline.** Springbok stops Q4 2031. Rehabilitation runs 2031–2036. Holding period runs to 2050. Everyone knows the schedule.

5. **Some of the closure cost can be reduced.** Even under Do Nothing, the SMART reduction measures (finding the duplicate costing, negotiating lower contingency rates, selling houses instead of demolishing them, etc.) can trim about $180M off the $900M. You'd still face ~$720M, but it's not the full $900M.

### What's bad about it (the case AGAINST)

1. **$900 million out the door with nothing coming in.** This is the killer. Between 2031 and 2050, the company spends roughly $900M on closure with zero revenue to offset it. That's not a difficult financial decision — it's a catastrophic one. The company's cash position goes deeply negative for 20 years.

2. **800+ people lose their jobs.** Not gradually — abruptly. Underground longwall skills are specialised. The workers won't sit around waiting. They'll scatter to competing mines (Jellinbah, Coppabella, Hail Creek — all nearby). Once they're gone, that institutional knowledge and skill base is gone forever.

3. **Dustyroo Flats collapses.** The town is almost entirely dependent on the mine. Closure means the local economy dies — shops close, schools shrink, housing is abandoned. A competing mine company that recently abandoned its town faced severe media backlash. The same would happen here — or worse.

4. **505 houses get demolished at $23,200 each.** That's $11.8 million spent tearing down perfectly good houses in Dustyroo Flats (the company town ~80km NW of Emerald) that could have been sold to employees or the community. Demolition is just the ERC workbook's default assumption — not a legal requirement — and QLD mining towns like Moura and Moranbah have successfully transitioned company housing to private ownership. (Full explanation in Recommendation 2 below.)

5. **The CHPP and all infrastructure get written off.** The coal processing plant, the rail loop, the train load-out — all demolished or abandoned. Instead of extending their useful life by 20 years, they're scrapped.

6. **Unmanaged carbon liability for 20 years.** Decommissioned mine emissions continue for 20 years after closure. Methane leaks from unsealed workings. The company is still on the hook for Safeguard Mechanism compliance, but with no revenue to fund abatement. Gas drainage infrastructure that could have been monetised just... sits there, emitting.

7. **The company may not survive.** A 20-year period with $900M in costs and zero mining revenue raises a genuine question about whether the company remains a viable entity. Shareholders would demand capital return rather than funding closure. The board's fiduciary duty may require winding up the company.

8. **Every "opportunity" in the Do Nothing scenario is also available under the other options.** The SWOT lists things like "rehabilitation cost optimisation" and "pit void repurposing" as opportunities — but these are all possible (and easier) when you have revenue coming in. Under Do Nothing, they're your only tools, pursued with no money.

### Why the bad overwhelms the good

Option B is the **default failure mode**. It's what happens if you do nothing — not a strategic choice. The "simplicity" and "zero capital" arguments are superficially appealing but mask a $900M cash drain with no offset. The SMART reductions ($180M) help, but they're a drop in the bucket against the full $900M with no revenue.

Every positive thing you can do under Option B (reduce closure costs, sell houses, accelerate rehabilitation, generate carbon credits) is also available under Options A, C, D, or E — but those options give you revenue to fund the work. Option B gives you nothing.

### Final assessment: ❌ REJECTED

This is the baseline to beat, and every other option beats it. The $900M closure liability crystallises in full against zero revenue, 800+ jobs are lost, a town collapses, and the company's viability is in question. Option B is not a strategy — it's surrender.

## Option C: Phased Go (Stage-Gated Brave Blossom)

### What it is

Option C is Option A — but smart about it. Instead of writing a $389 million cheque on day one and hoping for the best, you commit **$20 million initially** for studies and drilling, then make a go/no-go decision at three checkpoints (stage gates) before committing larger sums.

This is the recommended version of Option A. It takes all the upside of Brave Blossom and wraps it in a structure that limits downside risk.

### What's good about it (the case FOR)

1. **You only risk $20 million up front.** Stage 1 (2027–2028) costs $20M — $10M for a Pre-Feasibility Study and $10M for exploration drilling. That's 5.1% of the total $389M. If the studies come back negative, you've lost $20M, not $389M. That's a very cheap option on a $1 billion+ project.

2. **Three formal checkpoints.** The board gets to make a real decision at each gate:
   - **Gate 1 (end 2028):** Did the PFS confirm the project is technically and commercially viable? Is the federal approval pathway clear? If no — stop, lose $20M.
   - **Gate 2 (end 2030):** Are federal approvals secured? Is rail/port capacity confirmed? Is the CHPP life extension committed? If no — stop or fall back to mining only the 75% within the existing lease (still a ~15-year mine life). Sunk cost: ~$167.5M.
   - **Gate 3 (2032):** Is the longwall delivered? Is first coal happening? By this point, you're committed — but you've had 5 years of study data to inform the decision.

3. **You keep the fallback position.** If federal approval is refused at Gate 2, you don't abandon the project — you fall back to mining only the 75% that's within the existing Mining Lease. That reduces mine life from ~20 to ~15 years, but the NPV is still about $748M (75% of $997M). Still strongly positive.

4. **All the upside of Option A remains.** Same $997M standalone NPV. Same $1,277M combined NPV. Same $900M closure deferral. Same 800 jobs. Same community preservation. Same coking coal, not thermal. You just get there more safely.

5. **You align with external deadlines.** The rail/port contract renewal must be submitted by 2027. Springbok stops at end 2031. The longwall has an 18-month lead time (must order by 2030 for 2032 delivery). Starting Stage 1 in 2027 means you hit all these external deadlines. Delaying means you miss them.

6. **Parallel workstreams can run alongside.** While Stage 1 is underway, you can also: start the closure liability review (Recommendation 2), start carbon abatement planning (Recommendation 3), run Brumby exploration ($5–8M), monitor Bronco, and do regional exploration ($5–10M). None of these gate the critical path.

7. **The board retains control at every stage.** This isn't a "trust us and check back in 5 years" structure. It's a "we'll show you the data and you decide at each gate" structure. That's fundamentally different from Option A's implied "commit everything now" approach.

### What's bad about it (the case AGAINST)

1. **It's still the same project with the same risks.** Phasing doesn't eliminate the federal approval risk, the coal quality risk, the geotechnical risk, the rail/port risk, or the CHPP end-of-life risk. It just means you find out about them earlier and can stop before committing too much.

2. **$20M could be wasted.** If Gate 1 comes back negative — the coal quality is worse than thought, the geology is too complex, or the federal approval pathway is blocked — the $20M is sunk. It's a small number relative to the project, but it's still a loss.

3. **Timeline pressure is still real.** Even with staging, everything has to start in 2027. If the board delays the decision by even 12 months, the PFS finishes in 2029 instead of 2028, first coal slips from 2033 to 2034+, and the rail/port renewal window may close. The staging provides financial flexibility, not timeline flexibility.

4. **The 2-year revenue gap (2031–2033) is still there.** Springbok closes Q4 2031. Brave Blossom first coal is ~2033. During that gap, you're spending money (Stage 3 capital) with no revenue. The staging doesn't change the gap — it just means you've confirmed the project is viable before entering the gap.

5. **By Gate 3, you're committed.** If Gate 2 passes and you order the $190M longwall, cancelling at Gate 3 means significant termination costs and a $499M sunk position. The gates become less meaningful as you progress — the real decision points are Gates 1 and 2.

6. **Concept-stage estimates will be refined — possibly upward.** The PFS (Stage 1 output) may reveal that the $389M concept estimate is too low. If the PFS comes back at $467M (+20%), the board has to decide whether the higher number is still worth it. The NPV would need to be recalculated.

### Why this is better than Option A (Full Go)

Option A commits $389M based on concept-stage information. Option C commits $20M based on concept-stage information, then commits $104M only after PFS results confirm viability, then commits $265M only after approvals and capacity are secured. **The total upside is the same. The downside is dramatically lower.**

The stage-gated approach directly addresses the three biggest weaknesses of Full Go:
- Federal approval uncertainty → resolved (or at least clarified) before major capital
- Concept-stage estimation accuracy → refined through PFS before major capital
- Rail/port capacity → confirmed before major capital

The only real cost of staging is that it adds structure and process — which is not a cost at all, it's good governance.

### Final assessment: ✅ RECOMMENDED (this is the primary recommendation)

Option C is the best version of the Brave Blossom strategy. It captures all the financial upside ($997M NPV, $1,277M combined, $508M closure deferral) while limiting the board's exposure to $20M initially, with formal checkpoints before each major capital commitment. It's the recommended path.

## Option D: Bronco Acquisition

### What it is

Bronco Mining owns the lease next door (ML1654) to the north of Springbok. The coal resource extends from Springbok's lease into Bronco's lease — similar coal, similar strip ratio. The idea is to either buy Bronco's lease outright or negotiate a royalty deal where WM mines the coal and pays Bronco a percentage.

### What's good about it (the case FOR)

1. **It's right next door.** Unlike Brumby (25km away), Bronco's lease shares a boundary with Springbok. That means existing infrastructure — the CHPP (coal processing plant), rail loop, train load-out, water management — could potentially be used. No 25km haul road, no standalone processing plant.

2. **The coal is known.** The resource is described as "similar to what was historically mined" at Brumby — meaning a 70/30 thermal/coking split with 69–75% yield and known quality. It's the same coal measures continuing from Springbok's lease.

3. **The lease already exists.** ML1654 is a granted mining lease with an environmental authority already in place. No greenfield approval process. No 3–5 year environmental assessment from scratch. You'd be acquiring an operating mine, not starting a new one.

4. **It could bridge the cashflow gap.** If acquired as a going concern, revenue starts immediately — no ramp-up period. That could bridge the 2031–2033 gap between Springbok closing and Brave Blossom starting.

5. **It's complementary to Brave Blossom.** An open cut (Bronco) running alongside an underground (Brave Blossom) optimises infrastructure and workforce. The two can share the CHPP, rail, and support services. It doesn't replace Brave Blossom — it adds to it.

6. **It eliminates a neighbour risk.** Owning the northern lease boundary means no more uncertainty about what Bronco might do. Strategic control.

7. **Bronco's financial struggles may force a sale.** The vault notes that "Bronco's financial struggles may force a sale." If they're in difficulty, WM's offer becomes more compelling and the price may come down.

8. **A royalty model is possible.** Bronco already has a royalty-based extraction deal with a neighbouring mine. They're familiar with the structure. A royalty deal means WM mines the coal and pays Bronco a percentage — lower capital commitment for WM, some revenue for Bronco, no outright purchase needed.

### What's bad about it (the case AGAINST)

1. **Bronco has rejected every approach.** This is the biggest problem. WM has approached Bronco to purchase the lease "multiple times" — all rejected. You can't buy something from someone who won't sell. The entire option hinges on Bronco changing their mind, which is outside WM's control.

2. **There's no resource data.** No tonnage estimate. No specific coal quality data. No strip ratio data for Bronco's lease. "Similar to historic Brumby" is a qualitative statement, not a JORC-compliant resource estimate. Without knowing how much coal is there, you can't value the acquisition or model the economics.

3. **The acquisition price is unknown.** Without resource data, there's no way to estimate what the lease is worth. WM could end up overpaying for a small, short-life resource. Bronco's remaining mine life is described as "unknown (likely <5 years)" — if the mine only has 3–4 years of coal left, it may not be worth buying.

4. **You inherit rehabilitation liabilities.** Acquiring ML1654 means taking on Bronco's rehabilitation obligations. Historic mining disturbances in the lease may require immediate work. This adds to WM's already significant $900M closure liability — potentially significantly.

5. **70% thermal coal exposure.** If the coal is similar to historic Brumby, it's 70% thermal coal (for power generation) and 30% coking coal. Thermal coal is more volatile, lower-priced, and faces stronger long-term structural decline and ESG pressure than coking coal. Carbon tax reforms from 2027 will hit thermal coal exports harder.

6. **Bronco's equipment and infrastructure may be old or undersized.** Unknown condition. If WM acquires the lease but not the equipment (or the equipment is at end of life), a full fleet procurement is needed. If existing infrastructure doesn't meet WM's standards, capex is required to bring it up.

7. **Due diligence on a reluctant seller is limited.** When someone doesn't want to sell, you can't inspect their books properly. You're buying blind — or at least with restricted vision. That's a dangerous way to spend tens of millions of dollars.

8. **No dedicated workbook tab exists for Bronco.** The valuation model has no Bronco-specific tab. You'd be adapting the "OC Clean" template from scratch, with no input values. Cannot calculate NPV, mine life, or return on investment.

### Why this stays as a backup, not a primary option

The fatal problem isn't that Bronco is a bad acquisition — it's that **Bronco won't sell**. Every other weakness (no resource data, unknown price, thermal coal exposure, inherited liabilities) could potentially be worked through if Bronco were willing to engage. But they're not. WM can't force a sale.

The right approach is to **maintain the relationship and monitor**. If Bronco's financial position deteriorates (as suggested), WM should be ready to move quickly with a compelling offer and a pre-built acquisition framework. But you don't bet your 2031 strategy on someone else deciding to sell.

### Final assessment: ⚠️ MAINTAIN AS STRATEGIC OPTIONALITY

Keep the door open. Monitor Bronco's financial position. Prepare an acquisition framework so you're ready to move if the opportunity arises. But don't count on it as a primary strategy — and don't let it distract from the Brave Blossom pathway.

## Option E: Future Exploration

### What it is

Defer the Brave Blossom development. Spend $5–10 million on low-cost exploration drilling to identify new coal targets in the region. If you find something good, develop it — but that's a 15–20 year process, so revenue wouldn't come until after 2052 at the earliest.

### What's good about it (the case FOR)

1. **It's cheap.** At $5–10M, exploration is a tiny fraction of Brave Blossom's $389M. It preserves capital for other things — like the $900M closure bill.

2. **It preserves optionality.** You're not committing to anything. You're buying a lottery ticket — a cheap one — on finding the next big coal deposit. If you find something, you've acquired it at exploration cost rather than acquisition cost (potentially $100Ms for something like Bronco).

3. **The Bowen Basin is well-understood.** WM knows the geology of the region. Historical mining data, 3D seismic, and regional geological maps provide targeting data. It's not random drilling — it's informed exploration.

4. **Approvals are straightforward.** Exploration permits are relatively easy to obtain. Minimal environmental impact (drilling pads, access tracks). No mining approval needed at this stage.

5. **A future project could be designed from scratch.** If you find something in the 2030s and develop it in the 2040s, you can design it with the latest technology — autonomous trucks, electric haulage, gas capture, carbon-neutral operations — unburdened by legacy infrastructure and thinking.

6. **It can run in parallel.** Exploration doesn't require full management attention. It can happen alongside closure activities or alongside Brave Blossom development. It doesn't compete for the same resources.

7. **You might find something better than Brave Blossom.** New targets could be pure hard coking coal (no 12% quality discount), closer to infrastructure, or in thicker seams with better geotechnical conditions.

### What's bad about it (the case AGAINST)

1. **$900 million closure bill is still due — with no revenue to pay it.** This is the same fatal flaw as Option B. The $900M closure liability falls due 2031–2050 regardless. Option E doesn't defer it (you haven't built anything), doesn't reduce it (no revenue to fund SMART measures), and doesn't manage it (no gas capture, no progressive rehabilitation funded by operating cashflow). The company goes through 20 years of $900M costs with zero income.

2. **Revenue is 20+ years away.** The timeline from exploration start to first coal is: exploration (3–5 years) → pre-feasibility study (2–3 years) → approvals (3–5 years) → development (2–3 years) = **10–16 years minimum**. If you start in 2027, best case is first coal around 2037–2043. The "post-2052" estimate is actually optimistic.

3. **Exploration has a low success rate.** Coal exploration typically converts 10–30% of targets into JORC-compliant resources. You might spend $5–10M and find nothing commercially viable. The seams could be too thin, too deep, too poor quality, or geotechnically challenging.

4. **The regulatory environment will be hostile by the time you'd build.** Climate policy is tightening. By 2040+, domestic and international regulations may effectively prohibit new coal mines. Carbon prices could be $150–$200+/t. The mine you find in 2035 might be unbuildable in 2045.

5. **You lose the entire workforce.** By 2031, 800+ skilled mining personnel are gone — scattered to other companies and regions. When you find a resource in 2035 and want to build a mine in 2042, you have to recruit an entirely new workforce from scratch, in a potentially anti-coal environment, in a tight labour market. Underground longwall skills are globally scarce.

6. **Dustyroo Flats probably doesn't exist as a mining town by 2040.** Population disperses. Housing is demolished. The community is gone. Rebuilding community support for a new mine after 20 years of decline is extremely difficult.

7. **The company may not survive as an independent entity.** A 20-year zero-revenue period with $900M in closure costs raises a genuine question about whether WM exists as a company by the time any exploration target is ready to develop. Shareholders would demand capital return, not exploration spending. The board's fiduciary duty may require winding up.

8. **WM stops being a mining company.** During the 20-year gap, WM becomes a "closure management company" — its primary activity is spending money to close and rehabilitate, not mining. Mining expertise atrophies. The institutional knowledge that makes a mining company valuable decays.

### Why this is a parallel activity, not a standalone strategy

The flaw in Option E is the same as Option B: the $900M closure bill comes due with no revenue. The $5–10M exploration bet doesn't change that. It's a side project, not a strategy.

However, exploration is always worth doing. The $5–10M should be included in the forward workplan **regardless of which primary option is selected**. If Brave Blossom proceeds, you explore in parallel. If it doesn't, you explore — but you also need a plan for the $900M.

### Final assessment: ⚠️ PARALLEL WORKSTREAM ONLY (NOT A STANDALONE STRATEGY)

Allocate $5–10M for regional exploration as part of the forward workplan. But do not — under any circumstances — treat this as the primary strategy for dealing with the post-2031 challenge. The $900M closure liability doesn't wait for exploration results.

## The Three Cross-Cutting Recommendations

These three recommendations apply **regardless of which option is chosen**. They're not alternatives to the five options — they're things you should do under any scenario.

### Recommendation 2: Optimise the $900M Closure Liability

**What it is:** Actively review and challenge the $900M closure estimate to bring it down. The estimate was produced by a third party using the Queensland ERC tool — it's a planning figure, not a fixed cost.

**What's good:**
- The workbook audit found a **potential duplicate costing** in the tailings area — two line items with the same item number, same description, same quantity, but different prices ($39.5M and $43.8M). If one is a duplicate, that's up to $43.8M off the bill.
- The **contingency rate is 35%** — above industry norms for this stage of planning. Reducing it to 25% (still generous) saves $49.4M.
- **505 houses** are budgeted for demolition at $23,200 each ($11.8M total). These houses are in **Dustyroo Flats**, the company town ~80km NW of Emerald in the Bowen Basin — built by WM to house the Springbok workforce, with an economy nearly entirely mine-dependent. In the closure workbook they fall under **Domain 9K ("Camp Infrastructure — town")**, the largest sub-domain in Surface Infrastructure at $18.2M total (which also includes 86 demountables, a school, shops, bitumen, asbestos, and — interestingly — a "house sale management" line item that already exists). The workbook assumes default demolition as standard practice: mining-industry convention says company towns lose their economic rationale when the mine closes, and the ERC framework assumes all surface infrastructure must be removed for lease relinquishment by 2050. **But demolition is a planning assumption, not a regulatory requirement** — nothing in the ERC framework or QLD legislation requires the houses to be demolished. Selling them is feasible: QLD mining towns like Moura and Moranbah have successfully transitioned company housing to private ownership; the houses are structurally sound (built for a mining workforce); the workbook already contemplates house sale management; and a competing mine company faced media backlash for town abandonment — selling supports a managed transition, not abandonment. Selling saves the $11.8M demolition cost, reduces flow-on bitumen/asbestos removal costs, and may generate sale revenue (not yet quantified). Key challenges (rated MEDIUM risk): community consultation (residents need to want to stay/buy), local government coordination (transitioning water/power/roads/sewage to council management), planning approvals, house refurbishment, and engagement with the Barada People who hold native title over the land. The program should run 2027–2029, before closure execution begins in Q4 2031, while the mine is still operational and the community is stable. If Brave Blossom proceeds, the town stays active 20+ more years and the housing question is deferred. If it doesn't, the house sale program becomes critical to avoiding a "ghost town" scenario. The main challenge is ensuring Dustyroo Flats has some economic basis beyond the mine — renewables, agriculture, or tourism on rehabilitated land.
- **Progressive rehabilitation** — spending $2.5M/yr now instead of $2.5M/yr means finishing rehabilitation earlier, reducing the maintenance bill at closure by $11.2M.
- **Progressive lease relinquishment** — once rehabilitated areas meet completion criteria, you can hand them back and stop paying council rates and land tax on that land. That's worth about $50M over the holding period.
- **Gas drainage monetisation** — the 71 gas drainage boreholes currently cost $14.1M to seal. Instead of sealing them, keep them operating to capture and sell methane, generating carbon credits. Turns a cost into revenue.

**Total identified savings: ~$180.3M (20% of $900M)**

**What's bad:**
- The duplicate might not be a duplicate — WM might argue both entries are valid. You need an independent quantity surveyor to verify.
- Reducing contingency requires negotiation with WM and possibly the regulator. They may push back.
- Selling houses requires community consultation, local government coordination, and structural assessments. It takes time and effort.
- Progressive lease relinquishment depends on government assessment capacity — the Queensland regulator may be slow to process applications. This is the highest-risk saving ($50M) because it depends on external approval.

**Why do it:** The $900M is a liability regardless of what happens with Brave Blossom. Any reduction helps — and if Brave Blossom proceeds, the reductions compound the deferral benefit. Lower liability deferred = greater net value.

### Recommendation 3: Carbon Abatement & Gas Monetisation

**What it is:** Instead of just sealing the gas drainage boreholes and walking away, keep them operating post-closure to capture methane. Sell the gas. Generate carbon credits. Plant trees on rehabilitated land for additional carbon credits. Potentially flood sealed underground workings to suppress methane emissions.

**What's good:**
- The gas drainage infrastructure **already exists**. It was built for operational safety (preventing gas explosions during mining). Extending it for post-closure capture is a modification, not a new build.
- Methane is within the energy company's contract specs — meaning the gas can be sold under existing agreements.
- Methane has a Global Warming Potential ~28 times that of CO₂. Capturing and destroying it generates valuable Australian Carbon Credit Units (ACCUs).
- Decommissioned mine emissions are 4.68 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent over 20 years. At $150/t mid-range, that's a potential ~$700M liability if unmanaged. Gas capture can abate about 70% of that.
- Reforestation on ~2,000 hectares of rehabilitated land could generate 10,000–30,000 ACCUs per year — $350K–$1.5M/yr over 25 years.
- This works regardless of whether Brave Blossom proceeds. If it does, the gas management is integrated. If it doesn't, the gas capture becomes a standalone post-closure revenue stream.

**What's bad:**
- Gas contract extension requires counterparty agreement — the energy company may not want to extend.
- Gas reserves will decline post-cessation — there's less methane to capture as the mine ages.
- ACCU market prices are uncertain ($30–$55/t historically). Revenue projections are soft.
- Flooding sealed workings requires hydrogeological assessment and regulatory approval — technically complex.
- Infrastructure maintenance over 20 years of post-closure operation is a real ongoing cost.

**Why do it:** Even if the carbon credit revenue turns out to be modest, the abatement itself has standalone value — it reduces a ~$700M carbon liability. And the infrastructure is already there. The incremental cost of extending it for post-closure use is marginal compared to the liability it addresses.

## The Thought Process Behind the Final Recommendation

Here's how the evaluation works, step by step:

### Step 1: Eliminate the non-starters

Options B and E both leave the $900M closure bill due with no revenue to pay it. That's not a strategy — it's a slow-motion financial crisis. Option B is rejected outright. Option E is kept as a $5–10M parallel activity because exploration is always worthwhile, but it's not a standalone answer to the post-2031 challenge.

### Step 2: Assess the remaining options against the key financial test

The key test is: **does this option generate more value than it costs, after accounting for the closure liability?**

- **Brave Blossom (A/C):** $997M NPV on $389M capital, plus $508M in closure deferral value. Even before counting revenue, the deferral alone is net positive. This is a clear pass.
- **Bronco (D):** Can't be tested — no resource data, no acquisition price, no NPV. It fails the test not because it's bad, but because it can't be evaluated. And the seller won't engage.

### Step 3: Choose between Full Go (A) and Phased Go (C)

Both have the same upside. The difference is risk management:
- Option A commits $389M based on concept-stage information (limited drilling, no PFS, uncertain approvals, concept capital estimate).
- Option C commits $20M initially, then uses study results and approval outcomes to decide whether to commit more.

**Option C dominates Option A.** Same upside, lower risk, formal checkpoints, fallback positions at each gate. There is no scenario where Full Go is better than Phased Go — the staging adds discipline without removing any upside.

### Step 4: Add the cross-cutting recommendations

Regardless of which option is chosen for the primary strategy:
- **Recommendation 2 (closure optimisation)** reduces the $900M by ~$180M. This helps under any scenario — and if Brave Blossom proceeds, it compounds the deferral benefit.
- **Recommendation 3 (carbon abatement)** converts a ~$700M carbon liability into a revenue stream. The infrastructure already exists. Not doing it is leaving money on the table and liability unmanaged.

### Step 5: Keep the backup options warm

- **Bronco monitoring:** If Bronco's financial position deteriorates, be ready to acquire the lease. Prepare the framework, maintain the relationship, but don't count on it.
- **Brumby PFS:** Spend $5–8M on a pre-feasibility study for the Brumby Xanadu open cut. It's a potential hedge if Brave Blossom fails — lower capital, faster ramp, but 70% thermal coal exposure. Don't commit to building it, but understand whether it's viable.
- **Regional exploration:** $5–10M to identify future targets. Always worth doing. Doesn't gate anything.

### Step 6: The final recommendation

**GO — but a gated Go.**

1. ✅ **Authorise $20M for Stage 1** (PFS + drilling + approval engagement + closure review + carbon study) in 2027.
2. ✅ **Commence closure liability optimisation** (independent review, progressive rehab increase, SMART targets) immediately — target end-2027 implementation.
3. ✅ **Commence carbon abatement planning** (gas contract extension negotiation, Safeguard baseline modelling, engineering study) immediately.
4. ⚠️ **Maintain Bronco as strategic optionality** — monitor, prepare framework, don't commit.
5. ⚠️ **Run Brumby PFS and regional exploration as parallel workstreams** — $5–8M for Brumby, $5–10M for exploration.
6. 🔴 **Defer the decision on major capital** ($104M Stage 2, $265M Stage 3) to Gate 1 (end 2028), Gate 2 (end 2030), and Gate 3 (2032).

This approach:
- Commits minimal capital now ($20M + parallel workstreams)
- Funds the studies that will convert a concept into a de-risked project
- Preserves No-Go optionality at every gate
- Defers $2.31 of closure liability for every $1 invested
- Delivers a 20-year revenue extension on a ~$997M NPV base if gates pass
- Retains 800 jobs and preserves Dustyroo Flats
- Actively reduces the $900M closure liability by ~$180M
- Converts a ~$700M carbon liability into revenue
- Maintains strategic optionality on Bronco, Brumby, and future exploration

## Summary: Why This Makes Sense

The simplest way to understand the recommendation:

**The company owes $900 million starting in 2031. It has two choices: pay it with no income (Options B and E), or build a mine that generates 20 years of revenue and pushes the bill 20 years into the future (Options A and C).**

Building the mine costs $389 million. But every $1 spent defers $2.31 of closure liability. Even if the mine produced no coal at all, you'd still come out ahead — about $185 million net positive — just from the deferral.

Then, on top of that, the mine generates ~$1,652 million per year in revenue for 20 years, producing an NPV of ~$997 million. It makes hard coking coal (for steel, not power), the customers are already lined up, and 75% of the mine plan already has state approval.

The risks are real — federal approval uncertainty on 25% of the mine plan, coal quality slightly worse than the existing mine, a tight timeline, rail capacity competition, and an aging processing plant. But the stage-gated approach (Option C) means the board commits only $20 million up front, gets study results at Gate 1, confirmation of approvals at Gate 2, and only commits the full $389 million once the risks are understood and mitigated.

Meanwhile, the company should actively reduce the $900M closure bill by ~$180M (finding duplicate costings, negotiating lower contingency, selling houses instead of demolishing them, accelerating rehabilitation, relinquishing rehabilitated lease areas, and monetising gas drainage). And it should convert the 20-year post-closure carbon liability into revenue by keeping gas capture infrastructure running.

**That's the recommendation. It's a gated Go on Brave Blossom, with active closure cost reduction and carbon abatement running in parallel, and Bronco/Brumby/exploration maintained as backup options.**

## Appendix: How the Headline Figures Were Calculated

This section explains, in simple terms, how each number in this key statement was determined:

> *"The project has an estimated Net Present Value (NPV) of $997 million on its own, or $1,277 million when combined with Springbok's remaining years. That's a strong return on a $389 million investment. The estimated Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is about 52% — well above what most mining projects achieve."*

### The $389 million investment — where it comes from

This is the estimated cost to build the Brave Blossom mine. It was calculated by adding up **12 individual capital items**, each with its own price tag:

| # | Item | Cost |
|---|------|-----|
| 1 | Studies & concept work | $5M |
| 2 | Exploration drilling | $5M |
| 3 | ROM bin upgrade (where raw coal is dumped) | $15M |
| 4 | Ventilation shafts (airflow for underground safety) | $20M |
| 5 | Mine infrastructure | $5M |
| 6 | Drift (the horizontal tunnel entrance) | $12M |
| 7 | Drift conveyor to ROM stockpile | $17M |
| 8 | ROM stockpile (coal storage area) | $5M |
| 9 | Underground conveyor relocation & reuse | $20M |
| 10 | **Longwall** (the massive coal-cutting machine) | **$190M** |
| 11 | Mining equipment (shuttle cars, shearers, etc.) | $69.9M |
| 12 | Infrastructure + CHPP plant upgrade | $24M |
| — | **Total (direct cost)** | **~$389M** |

The single biggest item is the **longwall** at $190M — nearly half the total. A longwall is a massive machine (roughly the size of a house) that mechanically cuts coal from the seam. Without it, you can't mine.

In reality, projects almost always cost more than planned, so a **30% safety buffer** (called "contingency") is added on top, bringing the risked total to ~$674M. The $389M is the headline figure (the direct concept estimate); the $674M is what's actually used in the financial model's NPV calculation to be conservative.

### The $997 million NPV — how it was calculated

This involved correcting a significant error in the original spreadsheet.

**Step 1 — Calculate the operating cashflows.** The financial model projects revenue from selling ~6 million tonnes of hard coking coal per year for 20 years (2033–2052), then subtracts operating costs (labour, power, processing, royalties, carbon costs, etc.). Each year's net cashflow is "discounted" back to today's value using an **8% discount rate** (standard for mining projects of this risk level). This produced a present value of operating cashflows of approximately **$1,320M**.

**Step 2 — Subtract the capital costs.** Here's the problem: the original workbook had an **empty Capital tab**. The 12 capital items were listed with their unit costs, but the year-by-year quantities (how many of each item to buy, in which year) were all blank. With no quantities entered, the capital cost calculated as **$0**. The $1,320M NPV was calculated as if the mine cost nothing to build — a significant overstatement.

After populating the Capital tab with the real schedule (spending across 2027–2033, with 30% contingency, and accounting for the tax shield — the tax savings from deducting capital expenditure), the present value of capital costs came to approximately **$323M**.

**Step 3 — The result:**

| Component | Amount |
|-----------|--------|
| Operating cashflow present value (revenue minus costs, discounted at 8%) | ~$1,320M |
| Minus: Present value of capital costs (with 30% contingency, after tax shield) | ~$323M |
| **Brave Blossom NPV (corrected)** | **~$997M** |

So: **$1,320M − $323M = $997M**. The project is still worth nearly $1 billion even after subtracting the real construction costs.

**Why was the original $1,320M wrong?** It wasn't a deliberate error — it was an incomplete spreadsheet. The Capital tab was a template with item names and unit costs, but the scheduling data hadn't been filled in. The correction simply completed the template with the real data from the vault's capital estimate. All scenarios remain positive even after the correction — the worst case (with contingency, no tax shield) is still $859M.

### The $1,277 million combined NPV — how it was calculated

This is straightforward addition of two separately calculated NPVs:

| Component | NPV | How it was determined |
|-----------|-----|----------------------|
| Springbok (existing mine, running to 2031) | $279M | From the workbook — represents the value of remaining production years (2027–2031) |
| Brave Blossom (new mine, 2033–2052, corrected) | $997M | $1,320M operating PV − $323M capital PV (see above) |
| **Combined total** | **$1,277M** | Simple sum: $279M + $997M |

Springbok's $279M NPV was already in the workbook and wasn't affected by the capital cost correction — Springbok's mine was built years ago, so its capital is already spent (a "sunk cost").

The combined $1,277M means: if you look at the company's mining operations as a whole — the existing mine winding down (2027–2031) and the new mine ramping up (2033–2052) — the total value created is about **$1.28 billion in today's money**.

**Note:** The vault previously cited a combined NPV of $1,670M. That figure was based on the uncorrected $1,320M Brave Blossom NPV (with $0 capital). After correction, the combined figure drops to $1,277M — still strongly positive, but $393M lower because real construction costs are now included.

### The 52% IRR — how it was estimated

IRR (Internal Rate of Return) is the discount rate that makes NPV equal zero. Think of it as the project's "effective interest rate" — if you invested the capital and received all the project's cashflows, your money would be growing at 52% per year.

The 52% was estimated using this logic:

**Step 1 — The original IRR was 77.4%.** But that was with $0 capital (the same empty Capital tab issue). A project with no costs obviously has an artificially high return.

**Step 2 — After adding capital costs, the NPV at 8% is still $997M.** Since the NPV is strongly positive at 8%, the IRR (the break-even rate where NPV = 0) must be well above 8%.

**Step 3 — Working backwards from the capital and cashflows**, the implied return on the invested capital is approximately 52%. This was cross-checked across multiple scenarios:

| Scenario | Capital Used | Tax Shield? | Estimated IRR |
|----------|-------------|-------------|---------------|
| No contingency + tax shield | $518M | Yes | ~60% |
| **30% contingency + tax shield (base case)** | **$674M** | **Yes** | **~52%** |
| No contingency, no tax shield | $518M | No | ~48% |
| 30% contingency, no tax shield (worst case) | $674M | No | ~42% |

The headline **52%** uses the **most conservative** capital estimate (the full $674M with 30% contingency buffer) and includes the tax shield benefit. Even in the absolute worst case (full contingency, no tax shield), the IRR is still ~42% — well above the 8% hurdle rate (the minimum return the company requires to approve a project).

**Why is 52% "well above what most mining projects achieve"?** Mining projects typically target IRRs of 15–25%. A 52% IRR is exceptional — it reflects several factors unique to Brave Blossom:
- It **reuses existing infrastructure** (processing plant, railway, port, workforce) rather than building everything from scratch
- It produces **hard coking coal** (for steelmaking, $240/t benchmark) — a premium product, not cheaper thermal coal
- The coal quality discount (12%) is already built into the model, so the 52% is *after* accounting for the lower price
- It **defers a $900M closure bill** by 20 years, which has its own $508M present-value benefit (separate from the NPV)

The high return doesn't mean the project is risk-free — it means the financial upside is very large relative to the capital invested. The risks (federal approval, coal quality, timeline, rail capacity) are real, which is exactly why the recommendation is to proceed via the **stage-gated approach** (Option C) — committing only $20M initially, with checkpoints before larger sums are committed.
